## Detailed Program Overview *(baseline: ~500 words — trim or expand to match each funder's limit)*

Bottor Learning Foundation is a Wisconsin-based 501(c)(3) nonprofit whose mission is to advance literacy, innovation, and STEM through invention-based learning that empowers young inventors, creative problem-solvers, and future entrepreneurs. Founded on the belief that every child deserves the chance to identify a real problem, build something to solve it, and present their idea with confidence, the Foundation designs and delivers programming built on curiosity, creativity, persistence, and communication.

Our flagship program, the **Bottor Inventor Workshop**, is a five-day, hands-on invention curriculum for students in grades 3–5 (ages 8–11). Over the course of a week, students identify a real-world problem (Day 1: Discover & Imagine), sketch and begin building a solution (Day 2: Design & Begin Building), develop their prototype (Day 3: Prototype Development), test and refine it (Day 4: Test & Improve), and present it to parents, teachers, and community members at a culminating Inventors Expo and Pitch Day (Day 5). The workshop is guided by **The BOTTOR Framework** — Bring Out the Talent of Readers — our instructional methodology for weaving literacy into every part of the invention process, and by **Professor WiggleWoo**, the inventor-mentor character who leads students through it. Rather than treating STEM and literacy as separate tracks, every workshop integrates them: students read and apply an Innovation Vocabulary Challenge (6–8 words such as *prototype, innovation,* and *invent*), document their process in a Bottor Inventor's Journal, take on real team roles (Team Leader, Research Lead, Design Lead, Prototype Builder, Testing Lead, Pitch Presenter), and must correctly use at least five vocabulary words during their final pitch.

This request supports delivering the Inventor Workshop through Bottor Learning Foundation's modular delivery model: foundation-hosted cohorts during the summer or school breaks, in-school residencies with partner schools, library and community-center workshops, and after-school or weekend formats. The curriculum stays identical across every setting; only the delivery format adapts to the host. Funds support prototype-building materials, printed Inventor's Journals for every student, training for partner-site educators, and the culminating Expo where students showcase their prototype, journal, sketch, and pitch to an audience of parents, teachers, and community members.

Programming centers on students in grades 3–5 (ages 8–11), an age where students are ready to take on real research, design, and communication challenges while still building foundational literacy skills. We also support the teachers, families, schools, libraries, and community partners who help bring the workshop to students. Bottor Learning Foundation is headquartered in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and is building a long-term commitment to the Inventor Workshop statewide, with a goal of expanding to additional communities nationwide. Workshops themselves have no fixed location — they are hosted in partnership with schools, libraries, and community organizations throughout Wisconsin.

The intended impact is twofold: immediate — students leave the workshop with a completed prototype, Inventor's Journal, and pitch, along with a Certificate of Completion and measurably greater confidence in reading, problem-solving, creativity, and public speaking — and long-term, building the kind of early literacy and critical-thinking foundation that research links to sustained academic success (reading proficiency by third grade is one of the strongest predictors of long-term achievement).

## Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion

### How do you focus your programming and marketing to include underrepresented populations?

Equity and access are central to Bottor Learning Foundation's mission: we believe access to hands-on invention experiences should not depend on a family's income, zip code, or circumstances. That commitment shapes both where and how we deliver the Inventor Workshop.

We bring the workshop directly into the communities that are typically underserved by hands-on STEM and literacy resources, rather than requiring families to travel to us — partnering with schools, libraries, and community organizations embedded in those neighborhoods, and keeping partner-hosted sessions free to the host so cost is never a barrier to a school or library bringing the workshop to its students.

The workshop's team-role structure (Team Leader, Research Lead, Design Lead, Prototype Builder, Testing Lead, Pitch Presenter) is intentionally designed so every student can contribute according to their own strengths, welcoming students of all abilities and learning styles into a shared invention process.

### In the past 24 months, what advances has your organization made to ensure an inclusive environment (DEI workshops, training, etc.)?

Although Bottor Learning Foundation is an early-stage organization, we intentionally built diversity, equity, and inclusion into our mission from the beginning. Our programming focuses on increasing access to hands-on invention and literacy experiences for underserved communities, particularly children who face barriers to reading and to hands-on STEM opportunities. We are committed to creating welcoming, inclusive learning environments where every child has the opportunity to succeed, regardless of background, ability, or income.

During the past two years, we have prioritized community partnerships with organizations and venues that serve diverse neighborhoods, and we have designed the Inventor Workshop's team-role model to be accessible to children with varying learning needs and strengths. As we continue to grow, we plan to provide formal DEI training for our board and volunteers and will continue developing policies that strengthen accessibility and inclusion across all of our programs, including the accessibility technology initiatives we're exploring beyond the workshop.

## Customization Checklist for Future Funders

Before adapting this narrative for a new funder, check:

1. **Geographic eligibility** — does the funder restrict funding to a specific city, county, or region (rural vs. urban, in-state vs. national)? Confirm Bottor's actual service area matches *before* investing time tailoring language. (This is what ruled out Kohler Foundation — their focus is Sheboygan County and rural Wisconsin communities with limited other philanthropic capital; nothing in Bottor's current footprint matches that.)
2. **Program fit** — does the funder favor a specific angle (STEM, literacy, entrepreneurship/workforce readiness, arts, early childhood, etc.)? The Inventor Workshop's dual STEM+literacy design gives it a genuinely broad fit — emphasize whichever angle matches the funder, downplay the rest rather than forcing it in.
3. **Exclusions** — check for standard funder exclusions before budgeting: general operating support/overhead, capital campaigns or building projects, fundraising drives or event sponsorships, ongoing (vs. one-time) technology requests. Reframe budget line notes to make clear which category each cost falls into.
4. **Award size** — scale the budget model up or down to the funder's typical grant range or stated cap; keep category ratios consistent where possible.
5. **Named partners** — only name specific partner organizations (e.g., Vision Minds Entertainment, Kuumba Juice & Coffee for the Demo Dat Discography event) if that specific funder's project actually involves them. This master narrative intentionally excludes them since they belong to Bottor's supporting community-literacy programming, not the Inventor Workshop itself.
6. **Org address / mailing info** — resolved: 11414 W. Park Place, Suite 202, Milwaukee, WI 53224 (see Organization Mailing Address above). Use this for the application's org-info fields; don't substitute an event venue address.
7. **Participant/impact numbers** — the "4–6 cohorts / 100–150 students" estimate is a placeholder tied to this specific $10,000/$12,500 budget model, not a confirmed schedule. Replace it with the actual planned cohort count and size for the funder's grant period before submitting, and recalculate if the award size or program scope changes.
